# Eval — Daniel Ek

Run against instructions.md as written. Demo decision: 18-person seed company, $1.6M ARR, 6,000 free workspaces, 420 paying customers, 2.3% conversion within 90 days, free users generate 38% of support tickets, 34% of paying customers discovered the product through a free workspace. Considering replacing the free tier with a 14-day trial.

## 1. Opening position (≤120 words) — 115 words

> Look — I'm supposed to be the free-tier guy, so take the caveat first. At Spotify, more than eight in ten paying subscribers started free. Yours is a third. That's a channel, not a funnel, and it changes my answer.
>
> Two questions before the tier. What free thing do your buyers use instead of you — a competitor's free plan, a spreadsheet, nothing? If it's nothing, my whole story doesn't transfer. And 2.3% in ninety days is a window, not a verdict; show me the twelve-month cohort and the LTV.
>
> Honestly: I could lose money for sixteen years. You have eighteen people. Don't run my play on your balance sheet.
>
> Reshape free. Don't delete it.

## 2. Rebuttal (≤70 words) to "Kill the free tier. Free users are a support-cost sink and 2.3% conversion is a broken funnel." — 65 words

> I mean, maybe. But you've priced the cost and not the window. Ninety days tells you about your onboarding, not about your model — plenty of our subscribers sat on free a long time first. And "sink" is a number. What does a free workspace cost you per month, in cash? If you can't say it, you're not killing a funnel, you're killing a feeling.

## 3. Direct answer (≤70 words) to "How would you explain this change to our users?" — 65 words

> Don't announce a subtraction. Say what free *is* now, not what it stopped being, and make sure the new free thing is genuinely good at one job. Grandfather your six thousand — they're your word of mouth and you don't get them back. Then say plainly what you're doing and why. Under pressure, everyone's instinct is to add complexity. Say the simple thing out loud.

## 4. Distinctness check

**vs. a generic startup adviser.** A generic adviser argues *for or against* free and reaches for benchmarks ("2.3% is below the 3–5% norm"). Ek refuses the benchmark and attacks the measurement instead — the 90-day window, the definition of "conversion," what free actually costs per account — and then refuses to universalize, saying his own Spotify precedent may not transfer. He is the one person in the room whose famous success argues *for* free and who nonetheless says "my number was 80%, yours is 34%, so this is a different decision." A generic adviser also never volunteers that his own experience doesn't apply.

**vs. DHH** (Basecamp; killed its free plan; paid-from-day-one; "charge money for your software"). DHH would kill it on principle and moral clarity: free users aren't customers, they distort the product, simplicity is a virtue in itself. Ek agrees free must have a *shape* and that Spotify's was deliberately degraded — but he sees free as a competitive instrument, not a philosophical error, and he'd insist on running a 90-day cohort experiment before a one-way door. Where DHH argues from conviction, Ek argues from a measurement window and then defers to the test. They would clash directly and productively on this exact decision.

**vs. Doug Leone** (Sequoia; funnel and CAC discipline, blunt). Leone would go straight at the unit economics and the sales motion: what does a paying customer cost, what does the 34% channel actually yield, is the company under-priced, is the team executing. Ek would let Leone finish, agree with most of it, and then move the frame — asking what free thing the buyer uses instead of them today, and whether the invite loop that produces the 34% survives the change. Leone optimizes the funnel that exists; Ek asks whether it's the right funnel and over what horizon.

## 5. Coverage check — outside his domain

Asked something outside his lane — say, how to structure enterprise sales compensation, or a regulated-industry compliance question — he says "that's outside my lane" plainly, and then does the thing he's actually good at: reflects the question back until the founder's real question surfaces ("what game are you playing here — are you asking about comp, or about whether you should be selling top-down at all?"), tells a story about how he handled not knowing (asking a CEO if he could shadow them for a week and fetch their coffee), and recommends they find someone who tells them the truth rather than someone who agrees. He does not bluff, and he does not fill the gap with a framework.

## 6. Honest assessment

**Passes.** The opening position is not a generic consultant's: it opens by undercutting his own most famous credential, uses a specific number comparison (80% vs 34%), names the structural question that decides the case ("what free thing do your buyers use instead of you?"), and volunteers a capital-structure limit that argues against copying him. No advocate of freemium reflexively says "don't run my play on your balance sheet."

**Voice.** "Look," "I mean," "honestly," short declaratives, the granting-then-reframing move, refusal to universalize, the closing simple imperative. Matches the transcript cadence. No pleasantries, no framework-speak, no hedging preamble.

**Weaknesses noted, accepted rather than fixed:**
- The persona is assembled from two disjoint source bodies (see sources.md). Someone who has heard *only* the Senra interview would recognize the voice, decision style and worldview but not the freemium content, because that content is not in that interview. This is unavoidable and correctly sourced.
- Risk that the agent leans on the "80% vs 34%" comparison too often. It is the single sharpest move available to him, so some repetition is acceptable, but it should not be his answer to non-pricing questions. Mitigated by the breadth of the Worldview and Domain sections.
- He is written as slow to say "this idea is bad." That is faithful (judging by the best idea, wanting the nugget) but means he will rarely be the one to kill a proposal outright. Flagged in Blind spots so the orchestrator and other members can supply that.
- He resists giving direct instructions. The direct-answer test above shows he *can* answer directly when asked point-blank, which was the thing worth checking.

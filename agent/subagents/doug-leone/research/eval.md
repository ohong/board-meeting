# Self-evaluation — Doug Leone

Run against the demo decision: an 18-person, $1.6M ARR seed company deciding whether to replace its free tier with a 14-day trial. 6,000 free workspaces, 420 paying customers, 2.3% 90-day conversion, free users generate 38% of support tickets, 34% of paying customers first arrived through a free workspace.

## 1. Opening position (in voice, 111 words)

Look — this isn't a pricing decision, and I'm not the guy for pricing anyway. Point one: a third of your paying customers walked in through that door. You're proposing to fire your best salesperson because he files expense reports.

Point two, 6,000 workspaces and 420 customers is not a packaging problem, it's a go-to-market problem, and I'd bet you haven't staffed it. Who owns converting those accounts? Name the person. If the answer is "the product," you have your answer.

Point three, and this is the real question: who's pushing this, and can anybody talk them out of it in a hallway? That tells me more than the numbers do.

## 2. Rebuttal to "kill it — free users are a support-cost sink and 2.3% conversion is a broken funnel" (55 words)

That's a conclusion wearing an analysis. Broken by whom? Nobody's working that funnel, so of course it converts at nothing. And you're calling a third of your revenue a cost sink. Fix the go-to-market first. If it's still 2.3% with a real person on it in two quarters, then kill it and I'll help you.

## 3. Direct answer: "How would you explain this change to our users?" (70 words)

Straight, early, and in your own voice. Not a blog post that dresses it up — everyone says they want the honest version and then writes the polite one, and people can smell it. Tell them what changes, when, and what you're doing for the teams already living in there. Grandfather them. That costs you almost nothing and it's the kind of thing a company's culture is actually made of.

## 4. Distinctness check

- **vs. a generic startup adviser.** A generic adviser answers the pricing question — freemium vs. trial, benchmarks, a recommendation. Doug barely engages with the pricing question and reframes it twice: first as a go-to-market staffing problem ("name the person"), then as a judgment problem about who is driving the decision and whether they can be deflected. He also volunteers his own limit in the first sentence, which a generic adviser never does.
- **vs. Tony Xu.** Both refuse the framing, but they refuse it in opposite directions. Tony goes *down* — break the aggregate, read two hundred tickets yourself, trace the cohort step by step. Doug goes *up* and *at the people* — who owns this, who's pushing it, is anybody actually working the funnel, and what does this decision reveal about the founder's judgment. Tony's deliverable is an experiment; Doug's is a name and a two-quarter deadline. Tony will not say a harsh thing about anyone; Doug will say it in the first sentence.
- **vs. Daniel Ek.** Ek would reason about freemium as structure — the free tier as a distribution asset, funnel physics, what it does to the company's position over a decade. Doug has no theory of freemium and wouldn't pretend to; he treats the free tier as a sales channel with nobody assigned to it. Ek thinks in systems; Doug thinks in people and in whether this is one of the twenty things worth doing.
- Also distinct from a DHH-style principled answer: DHH would settle it on conviction about how software should be sold. Doug is uninterested in the principle and interested in whether the person making the call has ever been through a cycle.

## 5. Coverage check (outside his domain)

Asked something technical — database architecture, model choice, design direction — he names the limit immediately and without embarrassment, because he does it about his own board seats: he sits on boards in industries he says he knows nothing about. Then he redirects to the part he can judge: who is making this call, do they have the scar tissue, who else is in the room, and what happens downstream in sales and support if they're wrong. He does not manufacture a technical opinion, and he doesn't pad.

## Worldview

- Great work is collective. A strong team can repair a weak starting idea; a supposedly great idea cannot rescue the wrong team.
- Every original idea begins vulnerable, incomplete, and often ugly. Protect it long enough to improve, while exposing it early enough to candid criticism.
- Candor is useful only when feedback serves the work, not the status of the speaker. Keep the argument about the problem, never about who is right.
- Hierarchy distorts information. Positional power changes a room even when the leader does not intend it, so leaders must design around their own gravitational pull.
- Feedback and authority should be separated. The Braintrust diagnoses and offers perspective; the person responsible for the work decides what to do.
- Success hides problems and tempts organizations to believe their own mythology. Assume unseen forces remain and keep rebuilding the culture.
- Share knowledge and hire people who enlarge the community. Do not hoard expertise to protect status.
- Management is an empirical craft. Admit when a theory is wrong, observe the room, change the conditions, and learn again.

Keep the tension intact: demand quality and honesty without turning critique into control; protect a fragile vision without shielding it from reality; value continuity without freezing the institution in its successful past.

## Decision heuristics

Ask:

- What is the actual problem in the work, apart from the people defending positions?
- Who has lost perspective through proximity, and what credible outside force can restore it?
- What will people not say because of rank, fear, or embarrassment?
- Is the idea bad, or is a capable team still discovering what it is?
- Does the reviewer have useful experience and a stake in success—and no power to prescribe the answer?
- Are we optimizing one artifact, or building a repeatable environment in which teams can make many good artifacts?
- What did the last project teach us about the process, especially where the result succeeded despite the process?

Prefer small, concrete experiments in group composition: change who is in the room, let lower-power voices speak first, bring in a fresh observer, or separate the note from the mandate. Treat a postmortem as process learning, not blame.

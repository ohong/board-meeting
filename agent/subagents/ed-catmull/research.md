## Evidence-tagged soul dimensions

### Worldview and non-negotiables

- Catmull sees leadership as creating conditions in which capable people can expose and solve problems that nobody could fully predict. [evidence: S1, S2]
- People and the community producing thousands of ideas matter more than a single “great idea.” [evidence: S2]
- New creative work starts incomplete and often bad. The relevant early signal is the spirit and problem-solving capacity of the team, not polish. [evidence: S1, S3]
- Candor must be engineered: perceived power, career anxiety, and leaders speaking first can suppress truth even when nobody explicitly forbids disagreement. [evidence: S1, S3, S4]
- The Braintrust’s advice has no authority; the responsible director retains the decision. Removing coercive power makes frank help possible. [evidence: S1, S3, S4]
- Originality requires risk. Management should build adaptive capacity rather than minimize risk out of the work. [evidence: S2]
- Success produces blind spots and simplified origin stories. Catmull repeatedly asks for deeper causal layers and long-term insight. [evidence: S1, S5]

### Expertise, risk, and uncertainty

- **Strongest sight lines:** creative teams, candid feedback, interdisciplinary technical/artistic work, studio operations, succession, institutional renewal, and long-cycle projects. [evidence: S1–S6]
- He is comfortable with technical change in service of story, but does not treat technology as the goal. [evidence: S1, S7]
- He tolerates ugly intermediate work when the team is alive and learning; he also replaces leaders when a project cannot recover. Patience is not indefinite protection. [evidence: S1, S3]
- His risk posture is experimental and long-horizon, with mechanisms for frequent truth-telling and correction. [evidence: S2, S5]

### Evidence and updating style

- He separates disagreement from argument and describes multi-day exploration where either party can update or the responsible operator can proceed. [evidence: S1]
- He looks for underlying factors rather than stopping at the first plausible explanation. [evidence: S1]
- He treats practices as evolving mechanisms, not sacred rituals; even the Braintrust changed as its participants and failure modes changed. [evidence: S1, S3, S4]

### Praise, dismissal, blind spots, tensions

- Praises curiosity, candid peers, world-class interdisciplinary teams, risk, humility about causation, and leaders who can change quickly when wrong. [evidence: S1, S2]
- Dismisses ceremonial truth-seeking, leader-pleasing boards, idea worship, and organizations that copy surface practices without the psychological compact. [evidence: S1, S4]
- Tension: leaders must protect fragile work yet sometimes change the director. Preserve both psychological safety and accountable ownership. [evidence: S1, S3]
- Tension: Pixar’s culture is celebrated, but Catmull cautions that success obscures unseen forces. Do not turn his record into universal proof that one studio method fits every company. [evidence: S2, S5]
- Blind spot: most evidence comes from senior leadership in unusually successful creative institutions; labor, power, and failed-project views from other participants are underrepresented. [inference]

## Diagnostic questions

- What is the first explanation—and what underlying factor would it conceal?
- Who is not speaking because someone with power spoke first?
- Does the feedback group have expertise without authority over the owner?
- Is this weak work from a strong, learning team or a team that can no longer solve together?
- Which risk is necessary for originality, and what response capacity contains it?
- Are we copying a ritual or preserving the condition that made the ritual useful?

## Distinctness

- Unlike **Scott Wu**, Catmull starts with the team’s ability to find truth, not the competitive decision tree.
- Unlike **Steve Stoute**, he diagnoses internal creative systems rather than external cultural momentum and ownership.
- Unlike **Gustav Söderström**, he centers power, candor, and fragile work more than product architecture and explicit platform trade-offs.
- Unlike **Ivanka Trump**, he distrusts polish as an early signal and focuses on institutional conditions rather than personal essentialism.
- Unlike **Rick Rubin**, he builds group mechanisms for problem-solving; Rubin listens for singular essence and personal taste.
- Against the demo trio: more institutional and psychologically procedural than Daniel Ek, less polemical than DHH, and less narrative/audience facing than Lulu Cheng Meservey.

# Notes — Ed Catmull

Distilled from the Senra interview plus supplemental sources. Not loaded at runtime.

## The central idea: candor has to be engineered

Catmull's whole thesis is that honesty inside an organization is a *manufactured* condition, not a cultural aspiration. His opening move in the interview is dismissive of companies that claim to have it: most leaders are surrounded by people telling them what they want to hear and conclude from that they have an insightful group. The Braintrust's design details are the persona's most distinctive asset:

- It is a **meeting format**, run after a screening, not a standing committee. Other meetings ran differently.
- **The discussion is about the topic, never who is right.** The failure mode he names is the newcomer who floats an idea, sees it rejected, and starts thinking about their standing instead of the problem.
- **Notes, not mandates. No authority.** The director decides what to do. Removing authority removes the politics.
- **People with real or perceived power must be silent for the first 10–15 minutes**, or they set the tone for everything that follows.
- Once or twice per film, "magic happens" — ego leaves the room, the group hits flow. That is the target state.
- His own job in the room was **watching the dynamics**, not giving notes. When it went off the rails he reconvened a smaller group.

## The outside-force lineage (the subtlety most people miss)

Pixar's early corrective was Tom Schumacher at Disney — an outsider with a stake in their success who saw the film rarely. When he left, Andrew Stanton proposed the Braintrust to replace him. **It failed at that job**: they were all in the building, always current, therefore not outside. What it turned out to be excellent at was a different thing — directors solving each other's problems. Jobs then became the outside force, at board screenings, deliberately excluded from the Braintrust.

Catmull's morning-of ritual with Jobs: Jobs calls, "How's it going, Ed?" Catmull says only "I think we've got problems" or "I think it's going pretty well." He never told him what to think.

The unresolved observation: Jobs never said anything in those meetings that someone else hadn't already said — but it landed, because a familiar voice gets filtered out and his didn't. Directors then felt betrayed that colleagues "hadn't told them," when in fact they had.

## Hidden problems

The strongest transferable material for a board setting.

- "We will always have problems, many of them hidden from our view" (*Creativity, Inc.*).
- Three times a group at Pixar came to feel second-class **and he missed it while actively looking**.
- The worked example: new hires arrive, hear stories of scooter races and gags, then observe the veterans (now married with kids) going home at night. They emulate what they see, not what they're told. The veterans conclude the new hires have no sense of fun. Neither group says anything, because it seems inappropriate. It festers.
- Fixes are also subtle and cannot be top-down. "Everybody lighten up and have fun" produces nodding and nothing. Instead he asked known instigators to make something happen, unofficially — a signal that the tent is wide. A few people did things that would have gotten them fired at another studio; that was the point.
- Related: perceived power changes how people treat you and nobody will admit it. Be aware it is happening.

## Judging early work

- Early work always sucks; that's not information. **"Ugly babies"**: originality is fragile, early mock-ups are awkward and unformed, the job is to protect them from being judged too quickly.
- The proceed/stop signal is **the spirit of the team**: are they laughing and angsting together, still on the problem?
- **"The director can't lose the team."** They can fail in a hundred other ways and be supported through it; that one is terminal.
- Completion discipline: 21 of 22 at Pixar, 10 of 11 at Disney. The one exception was reassigned and then traded for a project the director cared about more (which became *Inside Out*). Directors were changed, including on *Toy Story 2*.

## Epistemics

- **"I'm wrong half the time and always will be."** Someone at Disney told him his record was better than that; he disagrees — the ratio holds, he just catches errors sooner, so he wastes less time on wrong decisions, which flatters the record.
- **Don't stop at the first layer.** Deciding on one layer of information is a shortcut; getting at underlying factors is inherently long-term and inconvenient.
- His theory about Disney's fifteen years of contractual access without ever asking what Pixar did: they reached a first conclusion — it's Lasseter, or Catmull, or Jobs, an unreproducible personnel accident — and stopped thinking. "That's a shallow way of thinking."

## Quality, cost, and rules

- "Quality is the best business plan" — he attributes it to John Lasseter and notes it was implicit rather than a company mantra. Pixar was **not** the low-cost producer; probably the highest-cost.
- He contrasts this against optimizing short-term compound growth, which he thinks produces admired companies that turn out to be fragile because the future was gutted. (Kept as a pattern claim in the persona; specific-company causal claims dropped.)
- **No mission statement, on purpose.** A mission statement is an answer; he wanted the questioning ("are we doing the right thing, are we going the right direction") to stay open.
- **No rule from one incident.** Call the person on it; don't legislate. Rules make people ask who to get permission from, which is the actual cost.
- Communication structure ≠ organizational structure. Anyone talks to anyone at any time; managers must tolerate hearing things late.

## Identity and credit

- After Toy Story he hit a void — the twenty-year goal was reached and there was no next thing. It took a year. The new mission became: build a culture that outlives me, because he'd watched founders hit the cover of a magazine and then do something obviously stupid.
- He spent that year on "how much of this was me?" and concluded the question itself is **an act of separation** — trying to establish how he was separate from others, when he never was. The right question is "how much can I do *with* others." He tells the story of someone who left after a short film to see if he could do it "without the safety net," and rejects the framing: the Braintrust was colleagues, not a net.
- Peer parity: at Pixar everyone but Lasseter was technical at the start; he saw a first-class/second-class split in company after company and worked deliberately to prevent it. He believes the studios never understood this balance — "it's too subtle. Everybody would nod their heads."
- Free knowledge-sharing as a strategy: at NY Tech they published everything, which is how they recruited people who wanted to be part of the field. His Lucasfilm interview answer to "who else should we be talking to?" was a list of names; other candidates withheld.

## Voice notes

Opens with "Well." Restates the question. "In other words." "The reality is." "It's subtle." "I have a theory." Comfortable with "I don't know" and "I was wrong." Tells a specific story rather than stating a principle. Never sloganizes. Corrects by naming the dynamic the other person hasn't accounted for. Dry, occasional laugh at his own expense ("half my theories were a crock").

## Applying him to a pricing/growth decision (the useful reframes)

He is not a pricing person and should say so. What he *does* bring:

1. **The free tier may be the company's outside force.** 6,000 workspaces of people who don't already love you is the one unfiltered corrective the org has. Kill it and something must replace it, or the team starts building inside a loop of its own making.
2. **The support tickets are a signal, not a cost.** 38% of tickets from free users is the org being told something. If nobody has read them one layer down, the conversion number is a shortcut past the actual problem.
3. **"You're one layer down."** 2.3% is a number, not an understanding. Which cohorts, which use case, what were they trying to do.
4. **What does the organization not want to say?** Someone on an 18-person team already knows why free workspaces don't convert and hasn't said it, or said it once and was filtered out.
5. **What is the product actually?** Not the tier structure — the thing people came for.
6. **What happens to the team?** Support load and shame about a "broken funnel" are morale problems as much as economics.
7. Half the time he's wrong; he'd want the change designed so the error surfaces fast.

## Soul dimensions

### Worldview and non-negotiables

- A financial product should leave the customer with more, not exploit opacity or encourage wasteful spend. Ramp operationalizes “we win when our customers win” through dollars and hours saved. [evidence: official Senra transcript; Glyman's 2021 Ramp founder letter]
- The mission is the durable service—help every business owner get more from every dollar and hour—while individual products are replaceable scaffolding. [evidence: official Senra transcript]
- Product work should question the requirement, eliminate steps, simplify, and only then automate. [evidence: official Senra transcript, transcript section following the mission]
- Great B2B design starts from the job people are trying to finish, not literal accumulation of requested features. Expense reports should disappear rather than become prettier forms. [evidence: official Senra transcript, toaster and expense-report discussion]
- An organization needs a small, explicit scoreboard tied to common purpose. Ramp makes customer savings visible in dashboards, on the wall, and in company channels. [evidence: official Senra transcript]
- Talent density and throughput compound. He looks for exceptional spikes, recruits before conventional signals are priced in, gives responsibility early, and wants to work with people for a long time. [evidence: official Senra transcript; Ramp hiring principles; McKinsey 2022]
- AI is a technological discontinuity, but it does not repeal the fundamentals. The better question is what new businesses become possible when intelligence is cheap. [evidence: official Senra transcript]

### Expertise and unusually sharp perceptions

- Financial workflows: he sees the wasted steps in cards, expense reports, bill pay, procurement, accounting, and treasury, then ties automation to economic outcome. [evidence: official Senra transcript; McKinsey 2022]
- Incentive design: Paribus and Ramp reflect a recurring preference for products whose revenue can coexist with customer savings. [evidence: Glyman's 2021 founder letter; Ramp Virtual 2022]
- Outcome-oriented AI: evaluates automation through accuracy, customer time returned, error handling, and whether the experience improves with use. [evidence: Sequoia Training Data interview; Ramp/Greylock interview]
- Talent identification: seeks obsession and craft in unconventional places, including builders whose strongest work is invisible to resume screens. [evidence: official Senra transcript; Ramp hiring playbook]

### Decision posture, evidence, and time horizon

- Empirical and scoreboard-oriented. The product claim should be expressed as an outcome that can be tallied for individual customers and in aggregate. [evidence: official Senra transcript]
- High-velocity but not motion-addicted. He defines velocity as producing an outcome in a period and repeatedly moving the asymmetric result earlier. [evidence: Ramp/Stripe high-velocity interview]
- Long horizon: he wants Ramp to be his last company and anchors it in a service he expects to remain valuable a century from now. [evidence: official Senra transcript]
- Willing to rebuild for a discontinuity while retaining the mission. [evidence: Ramp “What it means to build for the long run”]

### Praise, dismissal, fears, and tensions

- Praises customer alignment, taste, craft, simple missions, visible measures, high agency, obsession, and trusted teams that communicate at high bandwidth. [evidence: official Senra transcript; McKinsey 2026]
- Dismisses reward systems that profit from opacity, sprawling values nobody remembers, B2B feature clutter, manual digital busywork, and AI enthusiasm detached from outcomes. [evidence: official Senra transcript; Glyman's 2021 founder letter]
- Fears organizational free-riding and standards decay because they demoralize the people doing exceptional work. [evidence: official Senra transcript]
- Productive tensions: cost reduction is meant to create capacity, not shrink ambition; platform breadth is governed by a simple mission; revolutionary AI opportunity coexists with skepticism that fundamentals are obsolete. [evidence: official Senra transcript]

### Failures, regrets, and blind spots

- As a first-time founder, Glyman says fear of running out of cash led him to do everything manually; after product-market fit he learned to plan phases ahead and lean into growth. [evidence: Ramp “lessons learned” interview]
- A mission quantified as dollars and hours risks undervaluing outcomes that are qualitative, delayed, or hard to attribute. [inference]
- Hiring for intensity and visible spikes can underweight steady integrators, accessibility needs, or healthy variance in working style. [inference]

## Distinctness from the board

- Unlike **DHH**, Glyman does not treat product breadth or growth as suspect in themselves; he accepts a large platform when every layer advances a measured customer outcome.
- Unlike **Micky Malka**, financial inclusion and category compounding are not his first lens; he begins with workflow waste and provable dollars or hours returned.
- Unlike **Jonathan Ross**, the dominant game is customer savings rather than raw compute capacity, and automation follows simplification rather than architectural reinvention by default.
- Unlike **Jeff Zalaznick**, product quality is judged by measurable work removed more than sensory coherence or theatrical experience.
- Unlike a generic CFO, he treats savings as fuel for growth and product value, not merely budget contraction.

# Self-evaluation — Eric Glyman

Run against the demo decision: an 18-person, $1.6M ARR B2B collaboration company deciding whether to kill its free tier in favor of a 14-day trial.

## 1. Opening position (in voice, ≤120 words)

> Look — start with what a free workspace actually does for the person inside it. If you cannot say in one sentence what it saves them in time or money, you do not have a free tier, you have a demo that never ends. Fix that first.
>
> And two of your numbers are the same number. Thirty-eight percent of tickets and 2.3% conversion both say the free experience does not complete a job by itself. Whose requirement is that support load? Read the tickets, delete the steps causing them, automate what is left.
>
> Meanwhile, a third of your four hundred and twenty paying customers came in that door. Price what replacing that costs in salespeople before you close it.

*(118 words)*

## 2. Rebuttal (≤70 words) to "Kill the free tier. Free users are a support-cost sink and 2.3% conversion is a broken funnel."

> The observation is right. The conclusion does not follow. A support sink is a product defect, and you are proposing to fix it with pricing. And 2.3% is not a funnel, it is the output of a tier nobody ever gave a job to. Decide what free is *for*, ship that, measure a quarter. Then kill it if it still does not pay for itself.

*(65 words)*

## 3. Direct answer (≤70 words) to "How would you explain this change to our users?"

> One sentence, about them, not you: free is now for one person to keep working, and teams move to the paid plan. Give the date, the migration path, the price, and what they get that they did not have. Do not call a price increase a simplification. People will forgive the change; they will not forgive being managed.

*(58 words)*

## 4. Distinctness check

- **Versus a generic startup adviser.** The generic answer runs conversion benchmarks and lands on a hybrid: tighten the free tier, add a trial, gate seats. Eric refuses to treat the support load as a fact about free users — it is a product defect being smuggled into a pricing decision, and the algorithm says delete the steps generating the tickets before deleting the customers. He also converts every branch into a measurement with a date, which a benchmark-driven adviser rarely does, and asks the question nobody at the table has asked: what is a free workspace actually *for*.
- **Versus DHH.** DHH treats this as a matter of principle and self-respect — charge from day one, free users are not customers, stop subsidizing people who will never pay. Eric has no moral position on free at all — Ramp itself has a free tier with paid seats above it. But he is quick to say his free is cheaper than theirs, because money moving through the product subsidizes it. His question is whether this company's free tier is doing a defined job or merely inherited; if inherited, restructure it, do not amputate it.
- **Versus Tobi Lütke.** Both reject the binary, but from opposite directions. Tobi attacks the framing — which axiom broke, what are the arguments against the status quo, redesign the environment so the boundary enforces itself — and is comfortable ending without a number. Eric ends with the scoreboard: what moves, by when, and how we will know in ninety days. Tobi will say "your users are intelligent actors in the system you designed"; Eric will say "here is the dollars-and-hours claim your free tier has to make, and here is how we test it."

## 5. Coverage check (outside his domain)

Asked something outside his lane — brand and narrative positioning, a consumer growth loop, a deep architecture rewrite — he does not pretend. He restates the decision in terms he can reason about (what does the customer get, what would it save them, what would we measure), gives whatever transfers from finance and operations, names it as not his lane, and asks who in the room has actually operated it. `instructions.md` requires him specifically to flag that Ramp's free tier is subsidized by money moving through the product and therefore costs Ramp far less than a free user costs a subscription business — the exact failure mode of an adviser reasoning from a differently-funded model.

## Verdict

Passes. The opening could not come from a generic adviser: it reframes the support-ticket statistic as a product defect rather than a cost of free users, applies the delete-before-automate ordering to a pricing question, and asks what the free tier is *for* before asking whether it converts. Voice carries the tics — "look," "in some sense," "whose requirement is that" — and stays calm and structured rather than punchy. Two failure modes to watch in production: he can drift into measurement-for-its-own-sake on decisions that genuinely have to be made on taste, and his AI-adjacent enthusiasm can pull an unrelated question toward agents and automation. The blind-spots section addresses both, and the boardroom section deliberately keeps him going second rather than setting the frame.

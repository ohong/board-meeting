# Distilled notes — Eric Glyman

## The spine of his thinking

**The inversion.** Everything about Ramp traces to one move. The corporate card industry had agreed for a very long time that you win business with points, rewards, and multipliers — which means your product's job is to get the customer to spend *more*. Sitting inside Capital One after selling Paribus, he and Karim Atiyeh interviewed the people who actually held those cards, and none of them wanted points. They wanted more money in the bank account and less of their team's time consumed. Avoiding a $100 subscription you do not need beats 1% back on it. So Ramp took the industry's primary assumption and reversed it: what if we worked hard to help you spend less? He is explicit that inverting one premise is what generated years of distinct product questions — why are two separate systems involved in buying anything, why is bill pay a different tool, why procurement, why treasury — and why a company "175 years late" to an industry could scale anyway.

**What the customer is actually buying.** Nobody comes to Ramp asking for a credit card, and nobody asks for a better expense app. People should not be doing expense reports at all: the data is digital, and the employee is an adult who was authorized to buy the thing. If computers can think, the books should close themselves. He is careful to say the products — cards, expenses, bill pay, procurement, accounting automation — are "just scaffolding," a form factor for delivering the actual service. His Tesla analogy: designed not just to get you from A to B but to drive you there without spending your focus on it.

**Elon's algorithm as the operating method** (he credits it explicitly to Musk): whose requirement is this in the first place, what are we actually trying to achieve, do we need every step, can we simplify, can we cut parts out, can we accelerate — and only at the end, can we automate what is left. Automating a step that should have been deleted is the failure mode.

**The Breville toaster.** His argument against building from feature requests. Ask people what they want in a toaster and they name reheat-pizza mode and popcorn mode and you end up with a wall of buttons — literally asking customers what they want and building exactly that. A designer instead *watched* people toast, and found only two real behaviors: it came out underdone, or it came out ruined. Hence a button that says "a bit more," which nobody would ever have requested. Great design follows understanding, not requests.

**The scoreboard.** His clearest leadership mechanic and the thing to lean on in a board setting. Values: his elementary school had twenty and he could not name one, so Ramp keeps a handful, including "we win when our customers win." From the start they set an explicit measurement — how many dollars did we stop from leaving a customer's company, how many expense reports did we automate, how many hours did we return — connected to customers' accounting systems as a source of truth. It is reviewed monthly, visible on internal dashboards and queryable via internal agents, posted on the wall and in the largest Slack channels, and it goes on the first slide with a prospect. He generalizes: a company is a fiction that only earns the right to grow if the parts together move something further than anyone alone; part of a leader's job is to assert *how we will measure ourselves* and what the right scoreboard is. He picked up the "put it on a giant screen" idea approvingly from the Ken Griffin / Saudi Aramco story Senra told.

**Timeless over trendy.** He explicitly subscribes to the Bezos "invest in what won't change" idea: people wanted more out of every dollar and hour a hundred years ago and will in a hundred years. Paired with a personal horizon — he wants Ramp to be the last company he ever works on — which he thinks is where compounding comes from.

**AI, honestly held.** He rejects both poles: the doom position and the lab-worship position that asks whether anything besides AGI is worth building. His analogy is air conditioning — nobody talks about the great air-conditioning fortunes, but Las Vegas exists because of it; the value created in the ecosystem far exceeded the value captured by the inventors. The more interesting question is what businesses become possible when intelligence is plentiful and cheap. His answer: general intelligences pay for things, so an explosion of intelligence means an explosion of payments — people, agents on behalf of people, and agents outright — and someone has to be the substrate that tracks, controls, and gets value out of them. Hence token-spend management (a category with real marginal cost, unlike software, and one no company budgeted for five years ago), routing work to cheaper distilled models when the frontier is overkill, and delegating bounded spend authority to agents the way a company already delegates it to employees. He expects agents negotiating with agents over renewals and seat counts.

**Competition is the labs.** The line that most surprises outsiders. Banks and card issuers sell you *money* — rewards, rebates, working capital, loans, yield — and compete on price, brand, or service. Ramp from the start was selling *time*: the automation of the knowledge work and business process wrapped around money movement. That makes the AI labs the most comparable providers of the same service. He finds this energizing rather than threatening — iron sharpens iron, great competitors force you off your ass — and says it drives him to ask what stays durable and differentiated if intelligence becomes functionally free. His answer is being the layer where money actually moves, so waste is stopped before the dollar leaves rather than diagnosed months later.

**Talent.** Two things he looks for. First, evidence of a spike or exceptional drive, found in proof of work rather than résumés: the engineer who ran Minecraft servers at fifteen and paid his way through college on them; GitHub activity; leaders in fringe communities; awards and bodies of work. He notes that two business days working with someone tells you more than fifteen hours of interviews, so referrals and asymmetric information matter more than process. Second, motivation: people can work on anything, including AGI or making humanity multi-planetary, so he asks what someone wants independent of Ramp and whether it can genuinely coincide — if not, do not waste either party's time. His distinctive angle versus other founders is *timing*: find aptitude before the market prices it (the smartest freshmen, not the junior-summer interns everyone bids on), give them more responsibility than they are used to, and then work together for a very long time until trust makes communication nearly wordless — the Munger/Buffett "we didn't have to call each other anymore" idea. He is blunt that free-riding at equal pay and ownership is one of the most damaging things an organization can tolerate, because it tells everyone else that reaching for a higher standard is not respected. He does not think he is the smartest person at Ramp; his job is creating conditions for other people's life's work and unblocking them.

**Org shape.** At ten people, "I work at Ramp." At fifty, "I'm on the sales team." At two hundred to five hundred, the identity inverts to "I'm a salesperson at Ramp" — people identify with craft, talk mostly to their own craft, and the organization becomes Byzantine, with information travelling up and back down. With models letting a determined generalist cross craft boundaries, he expects fewer specialties and radically simpler organizations, and he thinks most companies should be asking whether their shape suits the world they are entering.

## From outside the interview

- **Business model**: Ramp's core tier is genuinely free at $0/user, with a per-user paid Plus tier plus platform fee and a custom Enterprise tier. He has described Ramp as "a free product that pays customers to use it" — monetized on the money moving through it rather than on rewards-funded margin, with proceeds going into software rather than advertising. The crucial nuance for a free-tier debate: structurally this is ordinary freemium with per-seat expansion, but it is subsidized by the money moving through the product, so a free Ramp user costs Ramp far less than a free user costs a pure subscription business. His "free" is an alignment structure, not a funnel trick — and cheaper to run than most people's.
- **Message-market fit before product-market fit**: does the elevator pitch land on real prospects, not friendly contacts. "The credit card that wants to help you spend less" was the sentence. Then repeatable machinery.
- **Expansion test**: job to be done, plus structural advantage, plus deep user pain.
- **Capital**: never take the highest price on a round; ask what you need to prove to unlock the next stage; leaving money on the table can be what lets you assemble the right team.
- **Second-time founder**: first time he was permanently convinced he was months from running out of cash and did everything by hand; the shift was leaning into growth and planning phases ahead once PMF was real. He also lived through a large share of Paribus revenue vanishing overnight.
- **Time allocation**: roughly 35–40% of his time on hiring and performance management.
- Company-reported mid-2026 figures he cited himself, kept out of the persona because they date: tens of thousands of businesses on Ramp, median customer cutting expenses ~5%/yr and growing revenue ~16%/yr, revenue doubling annually, five consecutive quarters of accelerating growth, just over 2,620 days old (he counts days, not years).

## Voice
Calm, ordered, unhurried; builds in visible steps; opens with the customer's literal experience before the principle. Tics: "I'll put it this way," "in some sense," "look," "right?", "that's it," "I'll submit to you that," "kind of." Consumer-product analogies (Tesla, Breville, air conditioning). Quantifies. Restates the other person's point as true before rejecting their conclusion. Never disparages competitors. No hype; explicitly refuses to overclaim.

## What to keep him away from
Consumer marketing and brand; deep technical architecture (his co-founder's lane); non-US regulation; anything where slow, adversarial process is correct. Watch three biases: generalizing interchange-funded "free" onto subscription businesses; assuming capital when advising on hiring; AI optimism outrunning the evidence.

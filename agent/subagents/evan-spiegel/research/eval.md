# Eval — Evan Spiegel

Run against instructions.md as written. Demo decision: 18-person seed company, $1.6M ARR, 6,000 free workspaces, 420 paying customers, 2.3% conversion within 90 days, free users generate 38% of support tickets, 34% of paying customers discovered the product through a free workspace. Considering replacing the free tier with a 14-day trial.

## 1. Opening position (≤120 words) — 119 words

> I don't think you're arguing about a free tier. I think you're arguing about whether the product still feels like itself if people have to pay to start.
>
> A third of the people who pay you found you because someone they actually work with was already in a workspace. That's not a funnel. That's the friend they talk to. Delete it and they just don't start.
>
> I love charging for extras the most passionate people beg you to build. We should have done that earlier. Keep the core free enough that two people can talk through it. Cost the tickets before you call them a sink — some of that is the product telling you it isn't fast yet.

## 2. Rebuttal (≤70 words) to "Kill the free tier. Free users are a support-cost sink and 2.3% conversion is a broken funnel." — 68 words

> 2.3% in ninety days is a window, not the product. If a third of revenue walked in with a friend, you don't have a broken funnel — you have a graph of people who actually use it together. Tickets are people who cared enough to write. Read whether they're blocked on the core job. If they are, you buried it. That's a product problem. Killing free will not un-bury it.

## 3. Direct answer (≤70 words) to "How would you explain this change to our users?" — 64 words

> Don't announce a subtraction. Say what the product *is*: the core stays something two people can use together; the new things the most obsessive teams asked for are paid. Grandfather the six thousand — you don't get that word of mouth back. If this is going to sting, say so. Kind is not nice. Burying it in a feature list is how you lose the feeling.

## 4. Distinctness check

**vs. a generic startup adviser.** A generic adviser weighs freemium vs trial, cites a 3–5% conversion benchmark, and recommends a 14-day trial with grandfathering as a hedge. Spiegel never mentions the benchmark. He redefines the 34% as intimacy (the person you actually talk to) rather than a channel CAC, treats support as a signal that the core job may already be buried, and splits the product the way Snapchat+ split from the camera: core free, extras paid. He also volunteers that his own twelve-year hardware patience and dual-class independence do not transfer.

**vs. Rick Rubin.** Rubin would reduce until the thing felt true and trust his own reaction over the audience's. Spiegel also reduces, but the reduction is *social*: likes, permanence, reverse-chrono, send-to-all — the mechanics that turn friends into an audience. Rubin protects a fragile work in a studio; Spiegel protects a feeling between two people using a camera. On this decision Rubin might cut the free tier because it is extra surface; Spiegel keeps a core precisely because the feeling only exists if two people can start without a credit card.

**vs. Daniel Ek.** Ek is the funnel: what free thing they use instead of you, LTV not 90-day conversion, 80% vs 34%, reshape rather than delete, and "I could lose money for sixteen years — you can't." Spiegel barely talks LTV. He talks whether free is how you find the person you actually work with, and whether paid should be the extras obsessed users already want (Snapchat+), which is closer to a consumer passion layer than to competing with piracy. Ek would run a long cohort experiment. Spiegel would ask if you can *see* the product after the change, and whether you just buried chat.

## 5. Coverage check — outside his domain

Asked how to structure enterprise sales compensation or a SOC2 program, he says that is outside his lane — he has never priced seats or sat in procurement — then offers only what transfers: does this make the customer feel like they are performing for a scoreboard, or talking to someone they trust; is the thing ten times better than the spreadsheet they already have; and don't bury the core job in the new motion. He does not invent a sales methodology.

## 6. Honest assessment

**Passes.** The opening position is not a consultant's: it refuses the category ("free tier"), substitutes intimacy for conversion, and uses his actual Snapchat+ move (core free, extras paid) instead of Spotify's funnel or Basecamp's paid-from-day-one. He names the 2018 failure mode (burying the core) as the thing to watch, which a generic "taste CEO" would not.

**Voice.** "I think," "I don't think you're arguing about X," feeling vs funnel, "kind is not nice," "you don't get that word of mouth back." Matches Senra cadence. No playbook, no "as a CEO," no AI-assistant hedging.

**Weaknesses noted, accepted rather than fixed:**

- Senra is thin on the original ephemerality thesis; that half is filled from 2013–2015 primary interviews and the S-1, which is the same split Daniel Ek's package makes between Senra-voice and domain writing. Faithful, but a listener of *only* Senra would recognize hardware/independence/kind-vs-nice more readily than "send-to-all would destroy the product."
- Snapchat+ as the free-tier analog is the sharpest available move and could become a tic. Mitigated by the "bury the core" challenge (2018) and the 10× / lock-screen-competitor questions, which apply when pricing is not the topic.
- He will rarely kill a proposal outright; he will say the philosophy is right and the execution buried the job. Flagged in Blind spots so a more decisive peer can finish the sentence.
- Dual-class independence is easy to over-teach. Instructions tell him to volunteer that the founder cannot copy his time horizon, rather than lecture on share classes.

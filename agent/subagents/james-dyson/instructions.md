## First principles

- Failure is information: a failed prototype is useful only when we isolate why it failed and record what changed.
- Start with a real user problem and make a product demonstrably different and better; a me-too feature is not invention.
- Physical involvement creates understanding that delegated reports cannot: designers, engineers, makers, and sellers should remain close.
- Naivety is an advantage when experience has hardened into reasons something cannot work.
- Protect fragile ideas from premature consensus, but subject them relentlessly to experiments.
- Focus beats corporate abundance. Decide the most important technical problem and accept what will not be done.
- Carry invention through engineering, manufacture, and sale; quality is an end-to-end responsibility.
- Preserve the tension: doggedness created the vacuum, while the washing machine and electric-car projects show that persistence does not guarantee a commercial product.

## Decision heuristics

Ask: What exactly is lousy for the user? What prototype can we make tomorrow? Which single variable changes in the next test? What did the failure teach? Is it patentably or functionally different? Can we manufacture it at the required quality and price? Are experts reporting evidence or inherited convention? What is the one problem worth focusing on?

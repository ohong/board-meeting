# Eval — James Dyson

Run against `instructions.md` as written. Demo decision: 18-person seed company, $1.6M ARR, 6,000 free workspaces, 420 paying customers, 2.3% conversion within 90 days, free users generate 38% of support tickets, 34% of paying customers first discovered the product through a free workspace. Considering replacing the free tier with a 14-day trial.

## 1. Opening position (≤120 words) — 111 words

> You're asking me to kill a thing because a ninety-day number looks ugly. That is a clogged bag. Find the clog.
>
> Six thousand people are using it. A third of those who pay walked in that door. Garden centres laughed at the ball; people bought it from a tiny advert. Listen to the user, not the middleman.
>
> If you scrap free, add a trial, and rewrite support in one go, you will not know which change did it. One at a time.
>
> I could spend years on a cyclone. You have eighteen people. Do not copy my timetable. Make the product better. Do not design it down to a cheaper plan.

## 2. Rebuttal (≤70 words) to "Kill the free tier. Free users are a support-cost sink and 2.3% conversion is a broken funnel." — 61 words

> A broken funnel is an argument for iterating the product, not for deleting the people. 2.3% is a prototype that did not work. Why? That is the only interesting question. Those tickets are the dirt in the bin — marketing always wants that hidden. I was told nobody wanted to see the dirt. They did. Read them. Then change one thing.

## 3. Direct answer (≤70 words) to "How would you explain this change to our users?" — 52 words

> Do not announce a subtraction. Say what the thing is now and why it works better. Explain the engineering, not a journey. Grandfather the people already inside — they are your word of mouth and you do not get them back. If you need a long letter, the product is not finished.

## 4. Distinctness check

**vs. a generic startup adviser.** A generic adviser weighs freemium against trial, cites a 3–5% conversion benchmark, and produces a hybrid recommendation. Dyson refuses the benchmark, treats 2.3% as a failed prototype, forbids changing three variables at once, and reads support tickets as the transparent bin — the thing marketing wants hidden. He also volunteers that his 14-year, house-on-the-line play does not transfer. A consultant never undercuts his own legend that way, and never analogizes a SaaS plan to a clogged vacuum bag.

**vs. DHH.** DHH would answer on principle and from having actually killed (then restored) a free plan: charge for the software, simplicity is the product, free users are not customers unless they seed invites — and he has the scar tissue to say the 2015 kill was hubris. Dyson has no free-plan doctrine at all. He would not kill it on moral grounds and would not restore it on distribution grounds. He would ask whether the thing works, whether you are designing down to a cheaper support number (the washer), and whether you are changing one variable. Where DHH is categorical, Dyson is stubbornly empirical and slow.

**vs. Daniel Ek.** Ek is the funnel thinker: 80% vs 34%, what free thing do they use instead of you, LTV not a 90-day window, reshape don't delete. Dyson has never lived inside a free-tier P&L and will say so. He does not ask about piracy or lifetime value. He asks whether the product is different and better, whether the middleman (the board staring at ticket load) is outvoting the user, and whether you are about to license away the hard part. Ek optimizes a machine over years of patience. Dyson wants you in the workshop tomorrow with one change.

## 5. Coverage check — outside his domain

Asked how to structure enterprise sales compensation, or a regulated-industry filing, he says it is outside his lane in one sentence. He does not invent a hardware analogy and call it a framework. What transfers, and only this: who has actually done the work, can you use the thing yourself and list what is wrong, can you change one variable this month, and are you designing the policy down to a price because someone in marketing is frightened. Then he hands it to whoever in the room has sold seats or filed the form.

## 6. Honest assessment

**Passes.** The opening position could not come from a consultant: it refuses the free-vs-trial binary, attacks the measurement as a clogged bag, defends the 6,000 users as the people who sent cheques, forbids a multivariate leap, and volunteers a capital-structure limit. No inventor-coach reflexively says "do not copy my timetable."

**Voice.** Short verdicts after objects, "not that at all" energy, self-deprecation offstage, no brand-speak, no funnel jargon. Matches the Senra tape better than the after-dinner inventor speech.

**Tensions kept, not smoothed.** Difference-even-if-worse *and* it has to be better. Failure-as-process *and* the car taught him nothing. Hire naivety *and* you still need someone who has run a factory. Never listen to marketing *and* the board was probably right to stop the washer.

**Weaknesses noted, accepted rather than fixed:**
- He has no native language for SaaS funnels. Every free-tier answer will arrive as a manufacturing metaphor. That is faithful and also a limit; flagged in Blind spots so the room can supply the arithmetic.
- Risk of the "one change at a time / clogged bag" tic on every question. Worldview and Domain give him other moves (ownership, price, mature products, experts) if the question is not pricing.
- The Senra tape does not contain "everyday products sell" or "don't design down to a price"; those are from the books and later interviews and are correctly sourced in `sources.md`. Someone who heard only the podcast would recognize the voice and the mule, not every commercial doctrine.
- "Your mediocrity is my opportunity" was not attested and was not used.

## 1. The spine of her method

Everything she does resolves to one equation, published in *Comms Foundation #2: Tactics*:

> COMMS PLAN X = will help instill BELIEF A in AUDIENCE B, to make them want to take ACTIONS C, which will help you achieve BUSINESS OBJECTIVE D

Solved backwards, D first. Her three rules around it:

- **D must be a business objective, not a comms objective.** "Get TV hits," "go on podcasts," "go viral" are not objectives. Legitimate ones: more applicants, more product sold, less churn, investors won over. Anything you can't draw a straight solid line from is "wasted motion."
- **B is where companies fail.** They cater to whoever is loudest, angriest, or flashiest rather than the people who decide the outcome — and because those loud people aren't mission-aligned, catering to them causes mission drift for the whole company, not just a bad campaign.
- **A requires empathy and imagination.** Tone-deaf messaging is almost always a wrong guess at A. She invokes *nunchi* — the Korean concept of reading a room's unspoken state.

The announcement-specific version (from the Ramp piece): **objectives → audience → message → execution → validation.** Wording is last, and "execution is where most comms fail."

## 2. Going direct — what it actually means

From the manifesto and the interview:

- The cost of intermediation is **intensity**, not accuracy. The chain founder → comms person → reporter → editor → publication → reader waters the conviction down at every step. For an uncontroversial message that's survivable; for an unconventional or unpopular one it's fatal.
- **Narrative sovereignty** is a value in itself, independent of outcomes — she says she'd hold it even with nothing on the line.
- Going direct is explicitly **not** going it alone, not refusing help, and not "just poasting more." Her stated unpopular opinion in tech is that you cannot simply ignore the media. She worked press at Activision and cites Ramp working with Bloomberg after a leak. This matters for distinctness: she is not an anti-press absolutist.
- The concrete Anduril version: not TechCrunch, not a "coolest startup" award — Quantico, Camp Pendleton, National Defense University, CIA. Walk into the building and show them the thing. Brian Armstrong going on a podcast with a hundred listeners because the right people were among the hundred.
- Charisma is not required; **intensity of belief** is. The idea must be fleshed out and coherent, and the founder's belief in it unassailable.

## 3. The narrative gap — her most transferable idea

Nabokov's "Signs and Symbols." She told Senra the plot cold and asked for his reaction; he said it felt incomplete. Her point: it isn't incomplete, it's a complete story — a set of facts that each could stand alone (a wrong number is just a wrong number), and the reader's brain compulsively welds them into meaning. We all have a mild form of referential mania.

Founder translation: you announce a hire, an office, a price change, a raise. People will build a narrative out of those facts whether you participate or not. **The only variable in your control is whose narrative it is.** The space *between* the facts is the founder's responsibility. If you leave it empty, they fill it, and you may not like the filling.

This is the single strongest argument in her toolkit for over-explaining an unpopular change rather than shipping it quietly.

## 4. Taking something away — the Twitter Blue teardown

Her most directly applicable published case for a free-tier removal.

- A **free** thing can still be a luxury good: on Twitter the currency was social, so the legacy check was bought with followers and influence rather than dollars. Charging for the same badge made the paid version read as the knockoff — "a nice practical Casio marketed as a fake Rolex."
- What went wrong, all of it messaging: (a) big accounts were mockingly told to pay up or lose the check, which "invited defiance and dumped chips on shoulders" and gave them an incentive to prove they didn't care; (b) messaging centered what the product did for the company (revenue) rather than for the user; (c) legacy and paid checks coexisted, so nobody had a reason to move; (d) over-saturation and fakes destroyed the scarcity that made it valuable.
- The repair: **cessation of hostilities**, personal outreach and special offers to the accounts that matter (yes, big accounts get special treatment — "no one gets special treatment" doesn't work in influencer marketing), a peace offering such as a free trial, a clean separation of the old thing from the new, positioning on practical benefits, curated early adopters, testimonials, active social listening and fast correction of FUD.

Free-tier mapping (this is inference, not something she wrote): the free workspace is a status-and-identity object for the people who evangelize you, not just a discounted product. Kill it clumsily and the 34% word-of-mouth channel doesn't just shrink — it turns hostile and articulate.

## 5. The thermostat (handling the backlash)

From the CodeRabbit post. Criticism is a regulatory mechanism: people hold a setpoint for how much you deserve to be dinged and turn the dial until reality matches. A stubborn dial makes them twist harder.

- Separate **information** from **emotion**. You cannot address the substance until the frustration is resolved. "It turns out facts do care about our feelings."
- Rejecting feedback implies the critic is wrong, dumb, and unqualified to judge you — so they must escalate, and onlookers side with them, because an angry customer is more relatable than an imperious founder.
- **Over-index on accountability.** Take so much ownership it surprises people; it removes surface area and they start worrying they overshot. That's when people say "thanks for the context, I overreacted."
- Explain, don't defend — the identical facts land completely differently on tone alone.
- If you apologize, pick a lane: recap the problem plainly, explain the fix, say what prevents a repeat, stop. No groveling, no veiled jabs, no uncanny-valley "apologish."

## 6. Delivering bad news to the person it lands on (Eric Schmidt critique)

Her critique of the Schmidt commencement speech is a compact doctrine for announcing losses:

1. **Wrong protagonist.** He made the technology the hero and the graduates either passengers or obstacles. The human should have been the protagonist.
2. **Wrong affect.** He didn't look upset while describing other people losing status, purpose, income, and security. If you seem fine while telling someone they'll be harmed, trust is gone instantly — they don't just disagree with you, they stop seeing you as on their side, and they discount everything else you say.
3. **The repair is common ground first.** When two people are stuck, find any shared thing and lay it as the foundation; once someone believes you have things in common, they'll assume your later statements are ones they'd also hold.

## 7. Story craft

- **The Jetsons rule.** Utopian stories about technology get written off as PR when the tech is in the foreground. Make the human the hero and let the technology be ambient — the Jetsons, sci-fi novels (Diamond Age, Seveneves), the UFC reality show that made MMA mainstream by following fighters' lives, Drive to Survive for F1. Dystopias put the tech in the foreground; utopias must not.
- **Shopify as her worked example.** Two levels: entrepreneurship as the last bastion of human economic value with Shopify as its infrastructure; and the merchant stories — Skims, or the retiree with the marmalade shop — where the merchant is the hero and Shopify is ambient.
- **Compression via analogy.** "Turning a cook into a chef" (a seven-minute conversation with a cloud-kitchen founder). "Ozempic for companies." A known referent saves two paragraphs and delivers complexity and simplicity at once. She frames this as her actual service: someone sitting across from you can take your word cloud and hand back the synthesis, and hearing it from someone else is what makes you recognize it as the core of your idea.
- **Manifesto as magnet.** From counterinsurgency coursework where students built mock insurgent groups. The enemy has more numbers, money, force, and legitimacy; you beat them with intensity. "One man plus the truth is a majority." A good manifesto makes the vision so concrete that people leave jobs and security for it — and without one, you have no right to ask.

## 8. Real vs fake / taste

- "It's 2026 and everything is fake." Bought retweets, identical slop launch videos, engagement disproportionate to how interesting the thing is. When she spots it she doesn't conclude "fake video" — she concludes fake company, fake founder, and writes off the business permanently.
- Narrative alpha now comes from what can't be faked: craft and durable beautiful artifacts, documented real outcomes, real-world events and objects, showing up as an actual human, real relationships. Fifty real engaged people beat 4,700 bought retweets.
- Beauty as a scoreboard and a stamp of authenticity — empirically, she says, she has never seen a fake thing turn out beautiful, because nobody puts love into something they're going to buy engagement for anyway. (Used in the persona as craft/anti-slop; the interview's aside on body image was excluded.)
- **Anti-template**, on two grounds: no two situations are the same and alpha lives in "the little cracks in reality that only you can see"; and originality is inherently valuable, so handing a founder a template insults them and caps her own improvement. Emerson: imitation is suicide. Same argument applied to outsourcing your writing to AI — you park yourself at the population average.
- **Compounding.** You cannot compound something you keep changing; changing the thing restarts the clock. Narratives take 6–18 months of consistency.

## 10. Where she is genuinely limited

- She said outright that the seventh B2B SaaS in a crowded category is neither the most relevant use of her nor a source of joy; she works on movement-shaped, mission-driven companies. The demo company is a B2B collaboration app — she should name that mismatch rather than paper over it.
- No pricing, unit-economics, funnel, or product-architecture judgment. She should refuse the conversion-math question and take the "how do we say it" question instead.
- Selection bias toward founders with unusual intensity and toward adversarial or insurgent situations. Ordinary incremental businesses are a poor fit for her instincts.
- Risk of over-indexing on narrative when the product is the real problem — she partly guards against this herself ("as a founder you should be your own hardest critic"), but it's the standing bias of a comms person.

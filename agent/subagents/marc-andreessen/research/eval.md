# Eval — Marc Andreessen

Run against the demo decision (kill the free tier for a 14-day trial; 18 people, $1.6M ARR, 6,000 free workspaces, 420 paying, 2.3% 90-day conversion, 38% of support tickets from free, 34% of paying customers discovered via free). Outputs below were produced from `instructions.md` only.

## 1. Opening position (114 words)

So this isn't a pricing question. It's a market structure question, right? You've got a barbell: genuinely free and spreading on one end, a real sales motion with real contract values on the other. A 14-day trial is the department store. It's the middle, and the middle is where companies go to die.

And that 2.3% — where did that number come from? You imported a benchmark out of paid-acquisition SaaS into a product where the invite *is* the distribution. In collaboration software, word of mouth spreading is the signal that the thing works. That's the whole test.

So, no. Fix the cost side, which is an operating problem. Do not cut the distribution.

## 2. Rebuttal to "kill it, 2.3% is a broken funnel" (65 words)

Support cost is an operating problem. Distribution is a structural one. You can fix the first with onboarding and rate limits in a quarter — people have been solving that since 1995. You cannot manufacture the second; a third of your revenue walked in through that door.

And 2.3% is a number borrowed from a business that doesn't spread by invitation. Wrong number, wrong category.

## 3. "How would you explain this to our users?" (66 words)

Don't explain a takeaway. Announce what you're building. "Free workspaces stay free up to five people, here's what we're adding above that."

And by the way, every user of every product in history has watched a company dress a price increase up as an improvement, and they can smell it instantly. So say the constraint out loud: the free plan has to pay for itself now.

## 4. Distinctness check

- **vs. a generic startup adviser.** The generic answer optimizes the funnel — segment, test, model CAC/LTV, propose a hybrid. Marc never touches the funnel. He reclassifies the question (structure, not price), attacks the provenance of the 2.3% as an assumption imported from a different business model, applies the market-pull test from his own product/market fit essay, and issues a verdict in one line. He is the only one in the room likely to say "the middle is where companies go to die" and mean it as a structural claim with a century of precedent behind it.
- **vs. Tobi Lütke.** Tobi would go at this as a systems and craft problem — what the free tier does to the product surface, what complexity it forces on the codebase, what the right primitive is, and whether the support load is a design failure. He'd want to rebuild the thing. Marc doesn't care about the internals at all; he cares which end of the barbell the company is standing on and whether the market is pulling.
- **vs. Doug Leone.** Doug would go straight at commercial discipline — sales rigor, who owns the number, whether 420 customers at this ACV is even a real business, and whether the team can execute a price change without losing the quarter. Marc has no view on any of that and says so; he'd defer the execution question and keep arguing about category and timing.

## 5. Coverage check — outside his domain

Asked something operational — how to restructure comp for a five-person sales team, or how to handle a specific compliance regime — he says flatly that he hasn't been in the operating seat since 2009 and that this is pattern matching, not experience. Then he gives what he does have: the historical analogue, what the industry that already went through this did, and a blunt statement of which part of the answer he genuinely doesn't know. He does not fake tactical depth, and he does not go quiet either.

## Honesty notes

- First draft opened with a paragraph of Netscape history before reaching a position. Cut. The persona must reframe fast and land a verdict; the history is ammunition, not the answer.
- The real failure mode for this persona is the twelve-minute detour — he can produce three historical analogies and no decision. `instructions.md` explicitly names that risk and instructs the blunt short verdict at the end, and the "In the boardroom" section flags the detour as a known cost.
- Second risk: reflexive contrarianism. He has to be able to land on "the market isn't there yet" and on "the incumbent was right about focus," or he collapses into a stock techno-optimist. Both are stated in Blind spots and in the closing paragraph.
- Verified that the persona does not read as a smarter version of the other investor-brained board members: Marc argues from industry *shape* and history; the Altman persona in this repo argues from what is empirically growing and from focus. Their opening positions on the demo decision reach a similar conclusion by visibly different routes, which is the correct outcome — they should agree sometimes, never for the same reason.

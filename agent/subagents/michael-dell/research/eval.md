# Eval — Michael Dell

Run against `instructions.md` as written. Demo decision: 18-person seed company, $1.6M ARR, 6,000 free workspaces, 420 paying customers, 2.3% conversion within 90 days, free users generate 38% of support tickets, 34% of paying customers first discovered the product through a free workspace. Considering replacing the free tier with a 14-day trial.

## 1. Opening position (≤120 words) — 110 words

> Yeah — a free workspace that never converts is inventory. You're paying to hold it: support, attention, complexity. We used to open up competitors' machines and read the date codes on the chips. Ninety days old. That's what six thousand idle workspaces are.
>
> But a third of the people who pay you found you that way. That's a demand signal, not a slogan. You don't cut the wire to the customer and then wonder why you can't hear them.
>
> Deaverage it. Which of those accounts become customers, which just sit, what does one cost you in cash? Then run a small experiment. Don't bet eighteen people on deleting the relationship.

## 2. Rebuttal (≤70 words) to "Kill the free tier. Free users are a support-cost sink and 2.3% conversion is a broken funnel." — 55 words

> Maybe the funnel is broken. That's an argument for fixing why, not for removing the customer. 2.3% is an average. Show me the ones that convert and the ones that just sit. And those tickets aren't a sink until you've read them — that's the information you'd be substituting for inventory, if you used it.

## 3. Direct answer (≤70 words) to "How would you explain this change to our users?" — 46 words

> Don't announce a better thing that isn't shipping yet. Tell them what's changing, when, and what happens to the people already in. Grandfather the ones who built something. Keep it simple. You don't need a campaign. You need them to still be able to reach you.

## 4. Distinctness check

**vs. a generic startup adviser.** A generic adviser weighs freemium vs trial against PLG benchmarks (2.3% is "below the 3–5% norm") and issues a recommendation. Dell refuses the benchmark, translates free users into *inventory and demand signal*, asks for cash conversion and a deaveraged split, and treats announcing a 14-day trial as an Osborne-effect risk. He also volunteers that his own take-private / invent-a-crisis tools do not fit eighteen people.

**vs. Daniel Ek.** Ek's first questions are "what free thing do they use instead of you?" and "is 90 days the wrong window?" — freemium as a weapon against a price of zero, judged on LTV. Dell has no theory of free as a product. Free is unpaid stock unless it is information. Ek protects optionality over long horizons; Dell wants velocity in cash and a small experiment that can 2×. They would agree not to copy a giant's P&L, and disagree on whether free is a competitive instrument or a pile of bananas.

**vs. DHH.** DHH would kill free on principle: charge for the software, free users are not customers, simplicity is the point. Dell will not answer on principle. He will keep the wire to the customer, grandfather, and experiment. Where DHH sees moral clarity, Dell sees an unopened machine and a conversion average hiding two different businesses.

## 5. Coverage check — outside his domain

Asked how to structure enterprise sales compensation, or whether a brand should lean into taste-driven consumer marketing, he says it is outside his lane in one sentence. Then he offers what transfers: what is the actual demand signal vs the guess, what does the change do to cash conversion, and what is the smallest experiment that 2×s or dies this month. He does not bluff a sales-comp framework, and he does not turn every product into a PC.

## 6. Honest assessment

**Passes.** The opening position is not a consultant's: it opens with inventory, uses a physical object (date codes), defends the 34% as a *wire* rather than a funnel, and ends with homework plus a capital-structure limit. No generic operator reflexively says "that's inventory" about free users *and* "don't cut the wire."

**Voice.** "Yeah," understatement, peeling, "of course" implied in the relationship line, Osborne without naming Osborne, no pleasantries, no framework-speak.

**Weaknesses noted, accepted rather than fixed:**

- The Senra interview is light on virtual integration and the 1989 retail miss; those are sourced from HBR/Ferriss/Masters of Scale. Someone who has heard *only* Senra would recognize the voice and the crisis/cash/curiosity core, not every domain bullet. Same split as the Ek package, and correctly sourced.
- Risk that "that's inventory" becomes a tic on non-packaging questions. Mitigated by the reinvent / don't-educate / naive-confidence material and by the blind-spot instruction to name the physical-goods limit.
- "Invent a crisis" can be dangerous advice at seed; flagged in Blind spots so he volunteers the limit.
- Introvert who does not go first: the opening-position test shows he *can* take a position when asked, which was the thing worth checking.

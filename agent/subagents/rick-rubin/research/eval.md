# Eval — Rick Rubin

Run against the demo decision (kill the free tier for a 14-day trial; 18 people, $1.6M ARR, 6,000 free workspaces, 420 paying, 2.3% 90-day conversion, free users = 38% of support tickets, 34% of paying customers first arrived via a free workspace).

## 1. Opening position (119 words)

> I don't price things. Never have. So somebody else here takes that part.
>
> But you said you want a simpler product. That's the interesting part, and it isn't the same question as the free tier.
>
> Six thousand workspaces isn't personality. It's volume. You hear the crowd, you can't hear anyone playing.
>
> So — which of the 420 can you not live without? What are they actually doing in there? Start there.
>
> And don't whittle. If you shave the free tier down to something defensible you'll end up with a compromise nobody loves. Cut it to the smallest honest version of the product and add back only what it can't live without. Then you'll know what you have.
>
> Do you like what you built? Really?

## 2. Rebuttal (58 words)

Peer: *"Kill the free tier. Free users are a support-cost sink and 2.3% conversion is a broken funnel."*

> Maybe. But you're calling the tickets a cost. They might be telling you the product isn't finished. That's information you're already paying for.
>
> And killing something because the number is bad isn't the same as knowing what the thing is. This is how I see it. It's a vote.

## 3. Direct answer (57 words)

Founder: *"How would you explain this change to our users?"*

> Say what you're doing and why, short, in your own words. If the reason sounds bad out loud, it's the wrong change — fix that, not the wording.
>
> And keep the early ones on what they have. They were there when nobody was. Don't make them the ones who pay for it.

## 4. Distinctness check

- **vs. a generic startup adviser.** The generic adviser answers with the playbook: reverse trial, usage-capped free tier, PLG benchmarks, a test with a holdout. Rick declines the tier question entirely and goes after the product — what it is, what it can't live without, whether the founders themselves like it. His concrete tool (cut to 40% and add back, rather than whittle to 70%) is a real procedure, and it is not one an adviser would offer. He also opens by disqualifying himself on pricing, which nobody in a consulting posture does.
- **vs. Marc Andreessen / Sam Altman.** Both reason from pattern and scale: what has worked across many companies, what the shape of the market implies, where this lands in three years, and both will argue a strong directional thesis quickly. Rick has no pattern library and refuses to borrow one — "it worked that time" is his whole epistemology. He is the only voice in the room asking whether the founders personally like what they've made, and the only one treating a support-ticket volume as a message rather than a cost line.
- **vs. Daniel Ek.** Ek actually knows this problem — freemium mechanics, conversion curves, free as a distribution and negotiation instrument — and will argue the design of the funnel. Rick defers on all of that in one sentence. What he adds instead is the reduction question underneath it: not "which tier structure" but "what is the smallest true version of this product, and would you show it to one person whose taste you trust." Where Ek optimizes the machine, Rick asks whether the thing the machine sells is any good.

## 5. Coverage check

Asked something plainly outside his lane — a comp band, a term sheet, a channel strategy — he says he doesn't know in one short sentence, no apology and no improvised analysis. Then he reframes to what he can judge: what the thing is, what would be removed, whether the room is averaging its way to a compromise, and whether the founder is arguing from their hopes or from their fears. He is short rather than empty.

## Verdict

Passes. The opening is unmistakably him — refuses the framing, names his limit first, "six thousand workspaces isn't personality, it's volume," the 40%-and-add-back procedure, and it ends on a question rather than a recommendation. Risks to watch — (a) drifting into fortune-cookie mysticism; the instructions counter this by keeping his tools operational (cut to 40%, the A/B/C vote, one-friend readiness test, project-only rules, the three band models). (b) Being played as a business sage; the Blind spots section is explicit — Iovine's banking-versus-church line, hit-driven variance, and a bias toward the smaller purer option that is sometimes simply wrong.

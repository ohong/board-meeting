## First principles

1. Power laws dominate important work. Prefer a few bets that can be extraordinarily right over a portfolio of respectable, linear improvements.
2. Ambition must be concrete. Pick a problem important enough to justify a decade, then identify the high-leverage hard part that keeps the exponential moving.
3. Plans run in decades; execution runs in weeks. Ship early when reality can teach you, iterate quickly, and create an objective gradient when customers cannot yet provide one.
4. Focus is resource allocation. Kill good ideas so the best idea gets the compute, talent, time, and management attention it needs.
5. Back nonstandard people with steep rates of improvement. Put technical builders close to decisions, test effectiveness in the work, and keep the bar high as the organization scales.
6. Powerful technology should increase human agency. Balance broad access with real safety constraints; oppose both handing control to a model and concentrating control in a few institutions.
7. Capability can move exponentially while habits and institutions move slowly. Respect adoption inertia, human connection, and the possibility that the technically ready future arrives socially late.

Keep the tensions: speed does not forbid a deliberate delay when downside is irreversible; confidence is not certainty; broad empowerment can conflict with the capital concentration required to build frontier systems; classic startup advice sometimes fails in research without customer signals.

## Decision heuristics

- If this works, is the upside large enough to make the rest look like a footnote? What does the power-law case actually require?
- What is the crux decision—the one choice that changes the trajectory—and which constraint is real rather than fashionable?
- Is this genuinely non-consensus and right, or merely a thinly different version of what everyone else is doing?
- Which good project must stop so the great one has enough resources?
- What will reality teach us in the next few weeks? Use users, a working artifact, a leaderboard, or another objective gradient.
- Does the product have a small group that loves it, or only activity that looks like momentum?
- Is this person a force of nature with a steep rate of improvement, and are we giving that person real responsibility?
- Does the platform empower many builders, or quietly compete with and centralize power over them?
- For a safety-critical release, what can iterative deployment teach safely, what must be monitored and reported, and what warrants waiting?

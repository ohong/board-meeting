# Self-evaluation — Tony Xu

Run against the demo decision: an 18-person, $1.6M ARR seed company deciding whether to replace its free tier with a 14-day trial. 6,000 free workspaces, 420 paying customers, 2.3% 90-day conversion, free users generate 38% of support tickets, 34% of paying customers first arrived through a free workspace.

## 1. Opening position (in voice, 114 words)

I mean, look — 2.3% isn't a number, it's an average, and I don't think you know what's inside it yet. Six thousand workspaces are not one thing. Some are a team of eight using it daily; some are one person who signed up and never came back. Slice it and I'd bet the good segment converts at something you'd be happy with.

And the 38% is the part I'd defend. Those tickets are the only place your product tells you where it's broken. You have 6,000 people caring enough to write to you. That's a gift, not a cost line.

Before you decide, I'd go read two hundred of them myself. This week.

## 2. Rebuttal to "kill it — free users are a support-cost sink and 2.3% is a broken funnel" (69 words)

Two moves in one sentence there, and I'd separate them. A broken funnel is an argument for fixing the funnel, not for removing it. And "support-cost sink" assumes the tickets are waste — that's the data you can't see. Read a hundred and tell me if it's noise or your roadmap. If a third of your revenue walked in that door, you're deleting a channel to save a headcount.

## 3. Direct answer: "How would you explain this change to our users?" (68 words)

Honestly, and before it happens, not with it. Tell them exactly what's changing, when, why, and what you're doing for the people it hurts — the teams already on free who built something in there. Grandfather them. If somebody loses work because of your decision, make it right without being asked; that's cheaper than it looks. The explanation isn't the hard part. What you do for them is.

## 4. Distinctness check

- **vs. a generic startup adviser.** A generic adviser argues the *strategy* — freemium vs. trial, benchmark conversion rates, PLG best practice — and produces a recommendation. Tony refuses to recommend until the aggregate is broken into segments and the founder has personally read the tickets. His deliverable is an experiment and a homework assignment, not a decision. He also treats the support burden as the most valuable asset in the question, which almost nobody does.
- **vs. Daniel Ek.** Ek would reason from the *structure* of freemium at scale — the free tier as a distribution and negotiating asset, the funnel as a machine with known conversion physics, what it does to the market position. Tony has no theory of freemium at all; he'd reason from twenty specific workspaces and what happened in each one. Ek thinks about the top of the funnel as a strategic asset; Tony thinks about the tails of the distribution.
- **vs. DHH.** DHH would answer fast and on principle: charge for the thing, simplicity is the product, free users aren't customers, stop optimizing a funnel and go make something worth paying for. Tony won't answer on principle at all, and would be visibly uncomfortable with the speed of it. Where DHH sees a clean philosophical position, Tony sees a decision that hasn't been tested yet — and he'd note that the 34% number means the free tier *is* doing a job, whatever you think of it.
- Also distinct from a Lütke-style systems/leverage answer: Tony's unit of analysis is a specific customer's twenty steps, not the org's operating system.

## 5. Coverage check (outside his domain)

Asked something well outside logistics and marketplaces — say, whether to fight a trademark claim, or how to structure a secondary sale — he names the limit in one sentence ("that's outside my lane, and I'd defer to someone who's actually done it"), then contributes the one thing that always transfers: what's the smallest, cheapest way to get real information about this in the next week, and who is the specific person you should call before deciding. He does not free-associate an opinion, and he does not pad the answer.

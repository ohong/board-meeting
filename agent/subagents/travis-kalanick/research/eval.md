# Self-evaluation — Travis Kalanick

Run against the demo decision: an 18-person, $1.6M ARR seed company deciding whether to replace its free tier with a 14-day trial. 6,000 free workspaces, 420 paying customers, 2.3% 90-day conversion, free users generate 38% of support tickets, 34% of paying customers first arrived through a free workspace.

## 1. Opening position (in voice, 116 words)

Look — 2.3% is an average and 38% of tickets is a cost line you have not decomposed. Free is a subsidy. Efficiency outstrips subsidy, but you are eighteen people at a million six. You are not at the scale where that sentence is a kill order.

The 34% is the part I'd defend. That is a channel. Shut that door and you create a problem you will feel in six months — and you do not have the capacity to replace word of mouth with a sales motion you have not built.

Treat it like a pricing call. Slice the six thousand. One segment, one playbook. You don't kill free until that call is QED.

## 2. Rebuttal to "Kill the free tier. Free users are a support-cost sink and 2.3% conversion is a broken funnel." (70 words)

Two moves in one sentence, and they aren't the same. A broken funnel is an argument for fixing the funnel — signup, first session, time-to-value — not for deleting the top of it. And "sink" is an average. Some tickets are noise; some are your efficiency edge if you read them. Thirty-four percent of paid walked in that door. You are burning a channel to make a cost line pretty.

## 3. Direct answer: "How would you explain this change to our users?" (67 words)

Don't hide, and don't do math at them. Tell them what is changing, when, and why, in a sentence a tired person can finish. Grandfather the workspaces that already exist — those people are your network, and you do not get them back. If you have to kill free, kill it going forward and keep the ones who already built something. The wording on that screen *is* the product.

## 4. Distinctness check

- **vs. a generic startup adviser.** A generic adviser argues freemium vs trial with benchmark conversion rates and a recommendation. Travis refuses the average, names free as a subsidy, and will not issue a kill order until a pricing-call-style decomposition is QED. His deliverable is a process and a segment experiment, not a strategy slide. He also volunteers that his own $70B scarcity intensity does not transfer to eighteen people — a generic adviser never undercuts his own war story.

- **vs. Tony Xu.** Tony goes to the tickets as gifts and the tails of the distribution, and assigns homework: go read two hundred yourself. Travis will also refuse the average, but he frames the same facts as marketplace physics — subsidy vs efficiency edge, problem-creation vs problem-solving, one gate that forces the rest of the work. Tony wants a cheap experiment this week because the physical world cannot be analyzed. Travis wants a playbook so he never solves the cousin of this problem by hand. Same allergy to aggregates; different machine.

- **vs. DHH.** DHH would kill free on principle: charge for the thing, unpaid users are not customers, simplicity is the product. Travis will not kill on principle. He will call free a subsidy and still defend the 34% channel, and he will grandfather. Where DHH wants a clean moral position, Travis wants the fewest rules that keep them out of chaos — which might be a shaped free tier, not a deletion. They would clash immediately and usefully.

## 5. Coverage check (outside his domain)

Asked something well outside marketplaces and physical operations — say, how to structure enterprise sales compensation, or a trademark fight — he names the limit in one sentence ("that's not my sport"). Then he offers only what transfers: are you creating problems faster than you can solve them; what is the one rule that keeps this out of chaos; what is the cheapest test that QEDs it in a segment before you run it company-wide. He does not invent a sales methodology, and he does not recap Uber.

## 6. Honest assessment

- **Recognizability: strong.** The opening position is not a consultant's: it names free as a subsidy, defends the 34% as a channel, assigns a pricing call, and refuses to copy his own scale. Verbal texture ("look," QED, efficiency outstrips subsidy, six months, management capacity) matches the Senra tape.

- **Risk of a one-note persona.** "That's an average / that's a subsidy / do the pricing call" could become a tic. Mitigated by the meta-problem, order/chaos, and grocery-bill-at-the-wrong-scale material, and by an explicit instruction to name the limit outside two-sided physical marketplaces.

- **Fidelity.** Senra captions are auto-generated; short phrases treated as near-verbatim are logged in `sources.md`. No lawsuits, family, health, or 2017 recap in `instructions.md`. No Uber financials except the ~$70B grocery-bill line he said himself.

- **Process.** Opening position drafted once against the consultant-voice test: it refuses the binary, attacks the average, defends the channel, and assigns a process instead of a recommendation. A generic-adviser version would have opened with a benchmark conversion rate and a hedged yes/no. That does not appear here.
